How to Start a Home-Based Food Business: Costing, First Customers and Food-Safety Basics

By FutureProof Blog · Published 26 October 2026 · 4 min read
In this article

  • Calculate the real cost per order
  • Test demand before buying in bulk
  • Check registration and local rules
  • A practical first-month plan

A home food business can begin with one dish people already ask you to make. The first challenge is finding out whether a small group will order it again at a price that covers the full cost. A long menu, new equipment and attractive packaging can wait until the numbers and routine make sense.

This guide is for someone considering a small, lawful food business from home. It cannot predict income. Sales depend on demand, location, safe handling, pricing and reliable fulfilment.

Start with a small menu

Choose one or two items you can prepare consistently. Set an order deadline and delivery window so you can buy and cook in batches. Record the portion size, ingredients, number of portions per batch, packaging and storage method. Weigh the finished portions and note anything wasted; failed batches and leftovers still cost money.

Check that the food can be prepared, stored and delivered safely. Keep the work area clean, separate raw and ready-to-eat ingredients, and communicate allergens clearly. Do not make health or disease-treatment claims for a dish.

Calculate the real cost per order

Ingredients are only part of the price. Add packaging, delivery or a delivery subsidy, payment charges, expected waste, and the time spent shopping, cooking, packing, answering messages and cleaning.

Here is a fictional example for one meal box:

Cost itemExample only
Ingredients₹55
Packaging₹12
Delivery support₹15
Payment and order costs₹8
Waste allowance₹5
Preparation and packing time₹25
Planning cost including your time₹120

The ₹25 time allowance values the owner’s work for planning; it is not automatically a paid or tax-deductible expense. At a ₹150 selling price, the remaining ₹30 must still cover fixed costs such as equipment and utilities. This is a pricing model, rather than an accounting profit calculation. Replace these sample figures with your receipts and time record.

Money left per order = selling price − ingredients − packaging − delivery − selling fees − waste allowance − your labour

If the result is negative, consider simplifying the recipe, adjusting portions, narrowing the delivery area or removing an item that costs too much to make.

Test demand before buying in bulk

Ask a small group of likely customers what they would order, on which day and at what price. A compliment is not the same as a paid order. Once the required registration or licence and local permissions are in place, take a manageable number of confirmed trial orders and buy only what you need.

Track orders fulfilled, expenses, time, cancellations, delivery issues and repeat requests. Keep customer details private, and do not use photos or feedback publicly without permission.

Check registration and local rules

Food businesses must complete the applicable registration or licensing before starting sales. For the specific “Home Based Canteens/Dabba Wallas” activity, the current FoSCoS eligibility table lists registration for annual turnover up to ₹1.5 crore and a State licence above that amount. Other activities, such as packaged-food manufacturing, have their own classifications; do not apply the home-canteen row to every home-made product. Select the category matching what you actually do and check its current requirements. The table is updated from 1 April 2026.

See FSSAI’s revised threshold order, the updated business eligibility list and the FoSCoS portal. Local municipal, housing, waste and delivery rules may apply too. Packaged products may have extra label requirements.

A practical first-month plan

Week one: choose the menu, test the recipe and calculate cost. Week two: complete the applicable registration or licensing and local checks before selling, then publish a short menu with portions, price and order deadline. Week three: run a small trial. Week four: review actual costs and feedback before repeating or changing the offer.

The first month is a learning period. Continue only if the food is safe, the routine is repeatable and the price leaves enough after costs and labour to make the work worthwhile.
