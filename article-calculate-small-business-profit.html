A busy day can still lose money. The number of orders tells you how much you sold; it does not tell you what remained after making, packing and delivering each item.

A simple pricing check can show whether a product or service contributes to the business or consumes your time. The example below uses fictional figures. Replace them with your own bills, receipts and time records.

Separate sales, contribution and profit

Revenue, for this simple worksheet, means completed sales after discounts and refunds, excluding taxes collected on behalf of government where applicable. It is measured before the business costs below.

Contribution per order is what remains after costs that rise with each order. The example below also deducts an assigned value for the owner’s time, so we call its result an adjusted planning contribution.

Profit is what remains after fixed costs and other expenses. Tax treatment, owner drawings, loans and one-off purchases may need separate treatment. This is a planning example, not accounting or tax advice.

Here ₹100 is the value assigned to the owner’s time. It helps test whether the price rewards the work involved; it is not automatically a cash payment or a tax-deductible expense. The remaining ₹150 is an adjusted planning contribution after allowing for that time.

At ₹150 per order after this allowance, 30 orders cover the listed monthly fixed costs of ₹4,500 while allowing ₹100 per order for the owner’s work. This is a pricing model rather than a statutory profit calculation.

That count assumes every order has the same price and costs. It excludes tax and expenses not listed. These figures illustrate arithmetic; they are not market-rate advice.

Count the work people forget

Small sellers often record materials but miss the time spent answering questions, checking stock, travelling for supplies, repacking a damaged item or correcting an order. Those minutes matter, especially when a price looks attractive only because the owner’s time is treated as free.

For each order, estimate materials, packaging, platform and payment charges, delivery, time for making and customer support, and an allowance for returns, waste or rework. Use recent receipts where possible. If you do not know the return rate yet, mark the assumption provisional and update it as orders come in.

A basic worksheet is:

Selling price − materials − packaging − payment and marketplace charges − delivery − labour value − waste or return allowance = adjusted planning contribution

Then:

Planning break-even orders = monthly fixed costs ÷ adjusted planning contribution per order

Round a fractional result up to the next whole order. Cash received is also different from profit: customer credit, stock purchases, loan repayments and equipment purchases can change available cash without matching that month’s profit.

If contribution is zero or negative, that formula is not a growth plan. Review price, scope, production time, delivery terms or product choice first.

Improve the offer before cutting the price

When the numbers are tight, look for work you can remove: combine nearby deliveries, limit unnecessary variants, standardise package sizes or clarify what a service includes. A discount can increase orders while shrinking the amount available to cover costs. Calculate contribution at the discounted price before launching an offer.

Review the worksheet after each batch of orders. Keep assumptions honest, update them when costs change and ask a qualified accountant about tax or accounting treatment for your situation.

Keep cash and fees clear in the worksheet

Start with the sale value before fees and record each charge once, or start from the net marketplace settlement and subtract only costs not yet deducted. Mixing those approaches can count fees twice. Keep taxes collected on behalf of government separate where applicable.

A product can show a positive planning contribution while cash is tight because stock was bought in advance or a customer has not paid. Maintain a second short list of cash received, bills due, refunds committed, customer balances outstanding and equipment or loan payments. Those entries explain why a bank balance and a profit figure can differ.

Questions readers often ask

Is money in my bank account profit?

No. The balance can include unpaid supplier obligations, customer advances, borrowed money or cash needed for refunds and tax. Compare it with the underlying records and upcoming commitments.

Does an owner-time allowance mean I paid myself?

No. It is a chosen value used to assess whether the work is worthwhile. Actual payments, drawings and their accounting treatment need separate records.

What if I sell several different products?

Calculate the contribution for each one. A combined break-even figure depends on the expected sales mix; do not use the highest-margin product to represent every order.

Should I discount to get more orders?

First calculate contribution at the discounted price and check whether the additional fulfilment capacity is realistic. A discount that leaves zero or negative contribution will not cover fixed costs simply by increasing volume in this model.
