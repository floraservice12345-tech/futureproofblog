FPI Inflows Hit ₹53,739 Crore in November 2026 — But Foreign Ownership of Indian Stocks Is Still at a 10-Year Low

By FutureProof Blog · 📅 Published 11 November 2026   🕐 10:20 AM IST   ⏱ 13 min read

The short version

  • Business Today reports ₹53,739 crore of FPI inflows in November 2026, the highest month since September 2024.
  • Foreign ownership of BSE500 companies is still 17.3%, a ten-year low, and 61% of large EM funds were underweight India in September (Jefferies).
  • Domestic institutions sold about 60 paise for every rupee foreigners bought in the first seven sessions of November (exchange data via Tapetide).

Where things stand on 11 November 2026: the Sensex closed at 81,106.65 and the Nifty 50 at 25,044.65 on Monday 9 November, an eight-month closing high and the highest since 27 February 2026. The exchanges were shut on Tuesday for Diwali-Balipratipada and reopened this morning. Gift Nifty was quoted at 25,329.50 at 8:05 am, about 285 points above Monday’s Nifty close, which points to a higher open. Behind the rally is a swing in foreign money: Business Today reported on 10 November that foreign portfolio investors have put ₹53,739 crore into Indian equities in November, the highest monthly figure since September 2024.

Most coverage stops at that number and calls it a vote of confidence. The positioning data published the same day says something more useful and a little less comfortable: the money is returning to a market that foreigners had almost vacated. This article reads the flow, the ownership data, the valuation and the domestic selling together, then turns them into a decision framework for an ordinary Indian saver.

Why the headline number depends on who you ask

If you read three November FPI stories you will probably see three different figures. They are not contradictions; they measure different windows on different bases. Here is every figure used in this article, with its source and what it covers.

Two things explain most of the gaps. The first is the cut-off date: a figure through 6 November will always be smaller than one through 9 November. The second is the data basis. Exchange provisional data counts cash-market trades by FIIs and FPIs and is published after each session. NSDL’s custodian-settled data arrives a day or two later and, as Craytheon notes, also captures primary-market buying such as IPOs. When you compare two sources, match the window and the basis first.

Business Today does not state the cut-off for its ₹53,739 crore, so this article treats it as a month-to-date figure published on 10 November rather than as a final November total. The month still has trading days left.

The number that matters more: foreign ownership is at a ten-year low

The instinctive reaction to “biggest foreign inflow in over two years” is that the opportunity has already moved. The positioning data points the other way. Jefferies’ India equity strategy note, reported by ANI on 10 November, puts foreign ownership of BSE500 companies at 17.3%, down from 19.6% in September 2024, which Jefferies describes as the lowest in the last ten years. A drop from 19.6% to 17.3% is 2.3 percentage points of ownership gone in just over two years.

The same note says that of 70 large active emerging-market funds managing a combined $370 billion, 61% were underweight India as of September 2026. The average fund was only 0.1 percentage points overweight India, against a ten-year average overweight of 2.2 points. In other words, the typical global fund holds roughly 2.1 points less of India than it normally does.

Put together, the picture is of a position that was cut hard and has only started to be rebuilt. Jefferies also reports that FPIs withdrew about $61 billion from Indian equities between September 2024 and October 2026, including $31 billion in the first ten months of 2026. The $8.2 billion that has come back since mid-October is a fraction of that. This is the point most headlines miss: the inflow is large against recent months, but small against what left.

What the arithmetic says about the size of the comeback

Two simple ratios, both worked from Business Today’s figures, show why the same number can sound large or small.

  • Against the last two months: FPIs withdrew ₹70,704 crore in September and October. ₹53,739 ÷ ₹70,704 = 76%. November has already returned roughly three-quarters of what the previous two months took out.
  • Against the whole year: Business Today puts 2026 FPI outflows at ₹2,41,406 crore, the largest for any year. ₹53,739 ÷ ₹2,41,406 = 22%. That ratio assumes the year-to-date figure does not already include November’s inflow, which the report does not say, so treat 22% as an upper bound for the share recovered.

Both statements are true. Whether you describe November as a recovery or a bounce depends on whether you measure from September or from January. The honest summary is a strong month in a weak year.

Why the money is coming back: what the named sources say

Business Today lists several drivers, and the strategists it quotes agree on the broad story while disagreeing on how durable it is. The cited drivers are concerns about whether large US AI investment commitments will earn adequate returns, volatile oil prices pushing central banks towards higher rates, the US midterm election outcome raising hopes of a slowdown in AI-led investment, stronger earnings momentum in India, and reports that the government is examining foreign investors’ concerns about capital gains tax. Business Today also calls India a “reverse AI trade” with reasonable valuations. The tax item is a report of something being examined; no change has been announced, and this article does not treat it as one.

  • Jefferies: moderating the concentrated AI trade could redirect allocations towards India; lower crude helps an oil importer; possible equity tax relief would improve India’s relative appeal; and “large caps could be the cleaner play on an FPI return.”
  • Vinay Paharia, CIO of PGIM India Mutual Fund: near-term risks from geopolitics, crude and interest rates are largely priced in, and large caps bore most of the recent reset.
  • SBI Securities: markets are risk-on, AI stocks have corrected and oil has pulled back, which helps rupee assets, but the Iran stalemate has no clear resolution and a reversal in yields is a live risk because domestic inflation estimates assume crude stays near current levels.

Crude oil is the other half of the thesis, and it has not been steady. HDFC Sky reported on 11 November that Brent touched a two-month low of $89.16 a barrel on Tuesday before recovering to about $92. Kotak Neo, citing Upstox, reported that Brent fell more than 16% in the week to 6 November. A rally partly built on cheaper oil is exposed to any reversal in the oil price. Check a current Brent price before leaning on any oil-linked argument.

The part the headlines skip: domestic institutions are selling

Foreign buying is only one side of the exchange data. Tapetide’s compilation of exchange provisional data shows DIIs were net sellers of ₹32,069 crore in the first seven sessions of November to 9 November, while FIIs were net buyers of ₹53,172 crore. Kotak Neo reported that DIIs sold a net ₹24,197 crore in the week to 6 November, ending an 11-week buying streak. Craytheon’s analysis of the same exchange data describes ₹10,445 crore of DII selling on 5 November as the largest single-day net selling by DIIs since April 2007.

Arithmetic on the Tapetide figures: ₹32,069 ÷ ₹53,172 = 60%. Domestic institutions sold roughly 60 paise for every rupee foreigners bought, so the net new money in the cash market was about ₹21,103 crore, not ₹53,172 crore. That does not make the rally weak, since domestic funds may be booking profits after a long buying run, but it does mean the move is a handover, not a one-way surge.

What this means for your money: a framework by reader situation

None of this tells you to buy or sell anything. It tells you which questions are worth asking. Here is one way to think about three common situations.

If you run a monthly SIP

The worry after an 8% weekly jump is that the next instalment buys less. Kotak Neo reported the Nifty 50 up about 1,977 points, or 8.6%, in the week to 6 November, so the index was near 23,070 before that move and is now near 25,045. Take a hypothetical ₹10,000 instalment. At 23,070 it buys 0.4335 index units; at 25,045 it buys 0.3993. That is about 7.9% fewer units for that one instalment.

Now spread it across a 36-month SIP, assuming for simplicity that the other 35 instalments each buy one unit-equivalent. Total units are 35 + 0.921 = 35.92 instead of 36, a shortfall of about 0.22%. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a forecast, but it shows the scale: one expensive month costs a long SIP a fraction of one percent, while stopping the SIP in a month like this forfeits every instalment you would have bought later, at whatever price. Our analysis of record SIP inflows looks at why monthly investing behaviour has been so persistent.

If your portfolio is heavy in IT stocks

The rotation described above is out of AI-concentrated markets, and Indian IT sits awkwardly on the wrong side of that story. On 9 November, HDFC Sky reported IT as the worst Nifty sector, down 1.2%, while PSU banks rose 3.7%. Kotak Neo reported Infosys and TCS ending the week to 6 November lower even as 46 of the 50 Nifty stocks finished higher. If most of your holdings are IT, the rally in the index may be doing less for you than the headline suggests. Our earlier piece on the September IT rally shows how quickly that sector can swing the other way, which is a reason to know your concentration, not a prediction.

If you are holding cash and waiting for a dip

Jefferies’ own view, that large caps look like the cleaner way to participate, is a positioning opinion with a stated rationale, not a recommendation for you. The more useful question is what your cash is for. Money needed within the next few years has a different job from long-term money, and a market at an eight-month high with average valuations is not an alarm either way. Our wealth framework sets out how to split money by time horizon before deciding anything about timing.

Is the market expensive after the rally?

Jefferies reports that the Nifty 100 trades at about 18.6 times estimated next-twelve-month earnings, roughly 5% below its ten-year average. Working back, that implies a long-run average near 19.6 times (18.6 ÷ 0.95), a figure derived from the report’s “about 5%”, not a number the report states. Kotak Neo, citing Upstox, put the Nifty 50 at about 19 times earnings. Those are different indices and different dates, but they agree on the broad message: valuations are close to their long-run average after a 9% month, not cheap. The stronger argument for India is not cheapness; it is an unusually thin foreign shareholder base and earnings momentum, and both can change.

What would change the picture

  • US AI spending sentiment. The reallocation depends on doubts about the returns on US AI investment. If reassurance returns, the flow can reverse.
  • Oil. Brent fell more than 16% in the week to 6 November and touched a two-month low on 10 November, according to the reports cited above, so any oil-linked inflow is exposed to a reversal.
  • US yields. SBI Securities flags a reversal in yields as a live risk. Higher US yields tend to pull money away from emerging markets.
  • Any tax change. A government decision on capital gains tax for foreign investors would matter, but nothing has been announced.
  • Domestic institutions. If DII selling continues while foreign buying slows, the cash-market support weakens.

What we could not confirm

  • Business Today does not give the cut-off date for ₹53,739 crore, so it is treated as month-to-date.
  • No source checked gives a full-month November return for the Nifty or Sensex. The 8.6% and 8.8% figures are weekly, to 6 November, from Kotak Neo; HDFC Sky says both indices have gained nearly 9% this month.
  • Reports that the government is examining capital gains tax concerns are unconfirmed and no change has been announced.
  • We did not find today’s closing levels, which will be published after the 3:30 pm close. The Gift Nifty figure is an indication, not a result.

Questions readers often ask

How much did foreign investors put into Indian equities in November 2026?

It depends on the source and the cut-off date. Business Today reported ₹53,739 crore of FPI inflows for November on 10 November. Exchange cash-market data compiled by Tapetide shows a net +₹53,172 crore for the first seven sessions through 9 November. HDFC Sky put it at $5.68 billion in dollar terms on 11 November, and Jefferies counts $8.2 billion since mid-October. These are different windows and bases, not competing estimates of one number.

Why do FPI numbers differ from one report to another?

Three things change the total: the last date included, whether the figure is the exchanges' provisional cash-market data or NSDL's custodian-settled FPI data, and whether primary-market purchases such as IPOs and block deals are counted. Kotak Neo, for example, reported ₹45,960 crore from exchange data for 2–6 November but ₹38,971 crore from NSDL-based data for the same week. Always check the window and the source before comparing two figures.

Is ₹53,739 crore a record?

Not a record. Business Today calls it the highest monthly figure since September 2024, when FPIs bought ₹57,724 crore. It also followed ₹70,704 crore of withdrawals in the previous two months, and the same report puts 2026 outflows as the highest for any year.

What does the “reverse AI trade” mean?

It is the description used in coverage of the Jefferies note: global investors who had concentrated money in AI-linked stocks are trimming that exposure, and markets such as India, with limited exposure to the big AI companies and what the note calls reasonable valuations, benefit from the reallocation. It is a view about where money is moving, not a guarantee that it will stay.

Does heavy FPI buying mean the market will keep rising?

No. Flows follow sentiment and can reverse as quickly as they arrived: foreigners were net sellers of ₹70,704 crore in September and October combined, according to Business Today. Jefferies and SBI Securities both name risks that could turn the flow, including a reassurance on US AI spending, oil and bond-yield moves, and the unresolved Iran situation.

Should I stop, pause or increase my SIP because of this news?

A single month of foreign flows is not, by itself, a reason to change a long-term SIP. The worked example in this article shows that even if one instalment buys about 8% fewer units after a sharp rally, the effect on a 36-month plan is roughly 0.2% of total units on the stated assumptions. If your goal, time horizon or cash needs have changed, review them for those reasons. This is general information, not personal advice.

What is DII selling and why does it matter here?

DIIs are domestic institutional investors such as mutual funds and insurers. Exchange data compiled by Tapetide shows DIIs were net sellers of ₹32,069 crore in the first seven sessions of November to 9 November while FIIs were net buyers of ₹53,172 crore. Kotak Neo reported that the first week's DII selling ended an 11-week buying streak. Domestic institutions absorbing part of the foreign inflow tells you the rally is not one-sided buying.

Is the Indian market expensive after the rally?

Jefferies reports that the Nifty 100 trades at about 18.6 times estimated next-twelve-month earnings, roughly 5% below its 10-year average. Kotak Neo, citing Upstox, put the Nifty 50 at about 19 times earnings. Those are different indices and the figures were published at different times, so treat them as a broad indication of “near the long-run average”, not a precise valuation call.
